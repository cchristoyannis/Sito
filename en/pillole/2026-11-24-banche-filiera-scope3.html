Banks look ten years ahead, supply chains are estimated from spending: the weak spot of reduction plans

Banks assess ESG risks over ten years, yet only 4% of companies measure Scope 3 with primary supplier data.

Few boards plan emissions reduction of their own accord.

In my experience plans are almost always drawn up because a rule requires them, such as the ESRS and, before that, the CSDDD; otherwise nobody wants to tie funds and resources to something they do not see as monetisable.

Banks look ten years ahead

Banks, meanwhile, look further ahead: European Banking Authority (EBA) guidelines require ESG risks to be assessed over at least ten years, and from 2027 the European Central Bank will review climate risk down to the individual client.

The weak spot is the supply chain

The weak spot is the supply chain. In my experience, companies act on Scope 1 and Scope 2, while on Scope 3 they manage only, or almost only, business travel and commuting, and estimate the rest from spending to avoid asking suppliers for data. EcoVadis and Kearney confirm it: only 4% of companies use primary data, and upstream Scope 3 may be underestimated by up to 2.7 times.

Measuring the supply chain with real data tells you where to act, and turns the plan from a compliance exercise into an argument for your next credit line renewal.

Data is not a cost, it is an asset.

My take

Companies often ask me what the return on a decarbonisation plan is. The most concrete answer comes from credit, even before the market: banks look ten years ahead and, from 2027, supervisors will go down to the individual client. A plan built on primary data from key suppliers is worth more than a stated target, because it shows where action is taken and with what effect. A few well-measured indicators are enough: emissions reduction, energy intensity, data from the suppliers that weigh most.
