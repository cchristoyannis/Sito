Natural capital is the next carbon footprint

Amazon funds leak sensors and wetlands in Germany to return the water it uses: those who measure their dependency on nature choose where to act, and banks are starting to ask.

When I started working on sustainability reports, the carbon footprint was a number few companies calculated and almost nobody asked for. Today it is the main challenge for any company.

On the starting line I now see natural capital more and more often: water, soil, air, minerals and living species. Every company depends on it and, at the same time, impacts it.

The Amazon case: water in Germany

Amazon shows it in Germany, with water. To return more water by 2030 than AWS uses, it funds leak sensors in 500 buildings in Frankfurt and the restoration of about 200 hectares of wetlands in the Spessart, expecting over 57 million litres a year by end-2027. These figures are the company's own, but they show how someone who has measured their dependency works: they choose the basin, the site and the intervention.

Harder to measure than CO₂

Compared with CO₂ the challenge is harder, because the water of a basin or the fertility of a soil are measured place by place and supplier by supplier. Banks are starting to ask: according to CRIF, 22.5% of lending to companies goes to firms with high impact and dependency on nature.

Two weeks ago we left the MBA students of Imperial and Luiss with a question: what if natural capital were the new carbon footprint? Amazon and lending data say it is already becoming one.

My take

Amazon did not wait for a rule to measure where it depends on water: it chose a basin, a site and an intervention, and declared an expected result. For a company the useful work is the same, even on a small scale: understand which resources its production rests on, site by site and supplier by supplier, and put a number on that dependency before the bank asks for it.
