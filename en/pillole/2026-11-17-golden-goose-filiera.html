Golden Goose and its supply chain: integrating and verifying suppliers

After the labour exploitation case in fashion, an example of a response in numbers: integrated suppliers, external audits including unannounced ones, checks down to sub-suppliers.

On Thursday I wrote about labour exploitation in fashion. Today, an example of a response, with numbers.

Integrating suppliers

Since 2022 Golden Goose has been vertically integrating its supply chain. Two footwear makers, in Casarano and Gricignano di Aversa. Part of production comes back in-house. No longer with subcontractors it does not control.

The audit figures

Some figures from its 2025 sustainability report:

• 96% of its roughly 190 suppliers are in Italy

• 87 on-site audits in 2025, over 350 since 2022

• covering about 100% of volumes, run by external auditors

• unannounced visits too, reaching sub-suppliers

• over 90% of suppliers at the two highest compliance levels

The cost of not checking

Not every brand can integrate its suppliers. But choosing a supply chain that is cheaper because it cuts corners, and not checking it so as not to see it, has a price. It is paid in trust in Made in Italy.

Made in Italy is not protected by a label. It is protected by creating value.

My take

Integrating suppliers protects two things at once: working conditions and the artisan know-how Italian fashion must pass on. But it is not a path for everyone, and on its own it is not enough.

Choosing the cheapest subcontractor without asking why it costs less only shifts the cost into the future: an investigation, a judicial administration, a brand that loses value.

The answer has to be collective. Italian fashion needs to work as a system: industrial districts that share rules, audits and data on subcontractors, and joint research hubs on materials, processes and artisan training. A district alone is no guarantee: Prato is a historic district, and many exploitation investigations start there. The difference comes when brands stop thinking as individual players and protect a common heritage together.

Either way, what matters is independently collected data that can stand up to stakeholder scrutiny and protect, over time, a strategic national asset like Made in Italy.
