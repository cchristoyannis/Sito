COP fails, the risk stays in the company

COP31 ends without a deal, yet European rules, banks and energy keep weighing on companies. Out of 75 Italian companies, none treats geopolitics as a material topic in its own right.

COP31 in Antalya closed on Sunday 22 November without an agreement. After two weeks of negotiations and two days of extra time, the final draft did not even mention fossil fuels, the European Union rejected it and the Turkish presidency had to acknowledge that there was no consensus. The reactions were of deep disappointment, starting with the United Nations.

One could file the news as yet another failure of climate diplomacy, far from the concerns of those who run a business. That would be a mistake, because the risk that diplomacy left open in Antalya does not stop there: it reaches the energy bills, the supplier contracts and the credit assessments of Italian companies.

European rules do not wait for a global agreement

The first point is that the rules that really weigh on our companies are European, and they move forward regardless of the COP. CBAM has entered its definitive phase and makes explicit the cost of the carbon embedded in imported steel, aluminium, cement and fertilisers. The ETS keeps cutting free allowances. On the credit side, the EBA guidelines require banks to assess ESG risks over at least ten years, and from 2027 the ECB will check how they manage climate risk down to the single client.

A missed global agreement suspends none of this. If anything, it widens the gap between a Europe that keeps pricing emissions and markets that are slowing down, with direct effects on the costs of those who import and export.

Europe, however, also disappoints on a decisive point. Alongside all these measures, through the Omnibus it removed the obligation to adopt a climate transition plan set by the CSDDD, which is the largest and most costly commitment for a company, but also the key measure of all, because the plan is where measured emissions become targets, investments and deadlines. I see the consequences up close: this week I reviewed all the rules already issued and in the pipeline to support a CSO who wants to convince the board to put resources into the transition plan, and who keeps hearing the same question: “which rule obliges us?”.

Geopolitics is an ESG risk that almost nobody measures

The second point concerns energy. According to the GreenItaly 2026 report by Symbola and Unioncamere, in 2025 Italy's dependence on foreign energy rose to 75.2%, with about 95% of gas imported. Every tension on a route, a supplier or an international price is therefore passed on to company accounts, often without the company ever having estimated how exposed it is.

In my experience this is where sustainability is still misread. Materiality assessments treat geopolitics as a context issue, while it is one of the most concrete risks for margins and business continuity. To test this I brought together 75 Italian companies: the 23 I have worked with directly over the years and another 52 listed companies whose 2025 sustainability statements I reread, from banking to energy, from transport to manufacturing. None treats geopolitics as a material topic in its own right. In ten cases it appears only as the cause of another risk, almost always energy costs, critical raw materials or staff safety, while in banks, energy networks and transport it stays confined to the pages on context. The European reporting standards, for that matter, never mention it, and ESMA did not find it either among the topics that 91 large European issuers added on their own initiative, where data, cybersecurity and artificial intelligence prevail.

Two levels of the same issue

On one side, banks, large clients and regulation will keep asking for data on emissions, energy and supply chains, with or without a COP agreement. On the other, measuring is the tool that shows where to act: which suppliers are most exposed, how much energy is used per unit of product, which share of costs depends on unstable markets. Reduced to a compliance exercise, measurement brings nothing; used as the basis for decisions, it cuts costs, protects margins and strengthens the dialogue with those who provide finance.

UN Secretary-General António Guterres called the Antalya outcome “deeply disappointing”, recalling that consensus may be missing in the negotiating room, but there is no ambiguity in the science. Disappointing results, however, add up. While in certain rooms nobody seems to worry about the consequences of their actions, companies keep paying every day, and dearly, for the indecision of institutions that keep changing the cards on the table, as with the Omnibus, or that fail to take serious decisions and commitments, as in Antalya.

In case it was not clear to everyone, indecision is the worst of decisions: it means deciding not to find a compromise, deciding not to commit to a strategy or a target, it means deciding to have no apparent responsibility, deciding not to care about the companies that pay for this context every day.
